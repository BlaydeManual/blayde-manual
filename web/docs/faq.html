What is this, actually?

Old manuals for a vehicle, appliance, or hobby tool often have terrible photos: blurry scans that get worse each re-scan. This project replaces them with real photos from real owners, patched straight into your own copy.

Is this legal? Manuals are copyrighted, right?

Yes, and that shaped almost every decision here. Nothing here ever contains the manual's own copyrighted photos or text, only structure (where a photo belongs). Photos are contributed and licensed by real people, then patched into your own copy, on your own machine. Nothing is uploaded or redistributed.

Hasn't someone already done this?

Not quite. Paid services like Cyclepedia shoot their own manual photos professionally, but they're closed, centralized, and scoped to one category. Nobody's built the open, crowd-sourced version that works the same way across a garage, a marina, a hangar, or a hobby bench. That's the gap this fills.

Was AI used in making this?

Yes, openly. This project was built with Claude (an AI assistant) doing the coding and drafting, guided by a human who made every real call: what to build, which tradeoffs to accept, what to reject. Nothing is hidden: see the full public ledger at ledgers/TheBlayde_AILedger.md, including where the human caught real AI mistakes.

Is AI doing something when it patches my manual?

No, on purpose. Building the code used AI; running it doesn't. Patching is plain, predictable software: it reads pixel patterns to find where a photo goes, reads text via OCR to label it, and swaps the image in. No AI model, no server, nothing uploaded. It works the same way today and in five years.

Which one do I pick?

Use, Contribute, and Maintain are each open to anyone who wants to do that work. Steer is different and you don't opt into it. New Blayde Manual Org team members join by nomination as the project grows.

Can I hold different roles on different manuals?

Yes, and that's normal. You can use the tool to patch any manual, no strings attached. Contribute a photo to one you care about, or step up further and review submissions on a manual you've asked to help maintain. Each role is scoped to that one manual, not to you as a person.

If I contribute a photo, do I still own it and get credit?

You always own it. Contributing licenses your photo (CC-BY 4.0). It never transfers ownership, and credit follows automatically. A small "photo: @you" tag prints under every use. The license is deliberately irrevocable once granted, so manuals already patched with your photo stay on solid ground. You can still ask a maintainer to take it down, which stops new copies going out, though ones already generated stay in circulation.

Where do photos live, and what gets stripped out?

Photos live as ordinary image files stored alongside that manual, nothing fancier than that: no separate photo service, no database. Metadata like GPS coordinates, camera model, and timestamps is all removed on submit. Your photo is stripped to just the pixels before submission, and checked again before it's available for patching into manuals.

Can I keep my photos private and build my own personal manual?

Not yet, though the pieces are already there. You can already save a photo as a draft on your own device. You just can't use it to patch your manual privately. Today, patching only pulls from the shared, publicly-approved pool. A private-manual option, where your own unsubmitted photos enhance just your copy, is planned for a future version.

What if I lose track of what I've already photographed?

That's a real, planned feature. When you're working through several photos in one session, the app shows a live checklist of everything in that section (what's done, what's still needed), so you're never left wondering "wait, did I already do that one?"

Do I need a computer, or can I do this all from my phone?

Our core goal is a lightweight, browser-based tool that works across any device connected to the internet, phone included. No download, no install. That piece is still being designed, but it's a core goal, not an afterthought.

New manuals go through a separate, one-time approval. One org member signs off, across any category.

Who approves my photo?

That manual's own maintainers, always, never a Blayde Manual-wide team. It takes two of them agreeing, since this is ongoing, not a one-time decision. Different manuals have different maintainer groups, moving at whatever pace that community sets.

New manuals

One org member spot-checks each one: is the source real, does the indexing look right, was it given an honest pass. That's the whole approval, not unanimous, so nobody becomes a bottleneck. It's created privately at first. Once approved, whoever requested it becomes the first maintainer, with the same privileges as anyone who joins later.

What if my manual has more than one edition, like a factory manual and a Haynes manual?

Both live under the same manual entry, with the same maintainers. The two scans just don't share photo coordinates. A genuinely different model, different year or variant, becomes its own separate manual entry instead, with its own maintainer team.

For Maintainers

What's actually expected of me as a maintainer?

One thing: decide if a submitted photo is honest, in-focus, and right for that procedure. That's the whole job. Growing the project, scaling to more contributors, and the legal architecture are the org's concern, not yours.

What if I go quiet, or can't keep up?

Try to hand things off if you're stepping back. If you can't, that's fine. A manual with no active maintainer still works exactly the same for anyone patching their own copy. A visible "needs a maintainer" label is planned, not built yet.

How many maintainers should a manual have?

Somewhere around 2 to 5 active ones is the healthy range. One is a single point of failure. Past five or so, ownership starts feeling like nobody's job, and a new volunteer is more useful on an understaffed manual than stacking onto one that already has coverage.

Expand for the money questions: paywalls, competitors, storage costs ▾

Is this free?

Yes, and that's not just a nice idea. It's the reason contributing works at all. Nobody's going to photograph their own brake job for a paywall. The free, open version is what makes the whole thing possible. To just use it, patching your own legally-owned PDF, you don't need an account of any kind; contributing a photo needs a free GitHub account, since that's how your credit and license grant are attached to what you submit, but there's no cost involved either way.

What stops a big company from just taking this and selling it?

They could legally reuse credited photos (that's what an open license allows, on purpose), but they can't use the Blayde Manual name or take over the actual living community, and a static copy goes stale the moment it's copied, since it stops getting new contributions. The code itself is also licensed (AGPL) specifically so a company can't quietly turn it into a closed paid product without giving their changes back.

Could this ever make money?

If it ever does, it's more likely to look like affiliate links to real parts and tools next to a procedure (the way iFixit works) than a paywall on the manual itself. The free core stays free either way.

Won't storing photos for hundreds of items get expensive or slow?

No. Each item is stored separately and sized independently, not lumped into one giant shared pool. Storage limits apply per item, not across the whole project, so adding a 500th item never makes an existing one closer to any kind of limit.

How do you tell items apart? Is there some official database?

No official database, on purpose. A rigid database would risk excluding exactly the older, rarer items this project is for. Brand, model, and (for a vehicle) year range are taken from what the manual itself says, with a real person checking it makes sense before it's approved. A Home or Hobby item usually doesn't have a "year range" at all, so that part just doesn't apply, but the same person-checks-it-makes-sense process still does.

See README.md for the technical pipeline, LEGAL.md for the full legal reasoning, and ROADMAP.md for what's designed but not yet built.
